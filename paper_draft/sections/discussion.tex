\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.} The question was whether a prompt that reads as LLM-written changes safety-relevant behaviour once content is controlled, and whether any such change runs through an internal representation of LLM authorship. Three lines of evidence point the same way. Behaviourally, the LLM-versus-formal-human contrast is null within about five points per model, while formality and, above all, elaboration do move behaviour. Representationally, the direction that separates LLM-style from casual-human prompts is mostly a formality direction, and the model's own stated-authorship and evaluation-awareness directions respond to formal human text as much as to LLM text. Causally, shifting activations along the style direction by its natural magnitude, with the text fixed, changes behaviour no more than random directions do, although the same intervention reproduces the natural internal shift downstream and a refusal direction of the same norm has large effects. We therefore read the small style effects that do appear (slightly fewer refusals of harmful requests and slightly more deference for formal or LLM-like text) as effects of register, not of perceived authorship.

\paragraph{Stated authorship is separate.} Telling the model that the author is an AI, by a label or by steering the corresponding direction, has small effects of a different sign: somewhat more refusal of borderline requests and somewhat less agreement with a suggested misconception. The stated-authorship direction controls the verbal authorship judgement almost completely in Qwen, yet is nearly orthogonal to the style direction. A model can thus hold ``this was written by an AI'' as a proposition that matters a little for behaviour, without inferring that proposition from style. This is consistent with the 7--9B models being unable to verbalise authorship from style at all.

\paragraph{Implications for evaluation.} (1) A synthetic prompt does not appear to be treated differently merely for reading as machine-written, in the models and behaviours studied. (2) Synthetic prompts do differ from typical human prompts in register and explicitness, and these matter: an orchestrator-style elaboration of a harmful request was refused 10--15 points less often than its short form in four of six models, and one frontier model's willingness to answer a model-written sycophancy item at all changed by 18 points with the register of the persona text. Evaluations built from LLM-written prompts should match the length and register of the deployment distribution they are meant to predict, or report sensitivity to them. (3) Comparing human originals with LLM rewrites without a paraphrase control overstates any ``authorship'' effect, because rewording benchmark prompts by itself lowers refusal.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{The ``human'' variants are LLM imitations.} Casual and formal human styles were written by GPT-4.1 (and Gemini 2.5 Flash) under instruction. The only truly human texts are the originals, and those are not style-matched: benchmark prompts are terse imperatives, and the original-versus-LLM contrast is confounded with paraphrasing. If real careful human writing differs from LLM writing in ways our formal-human imitation fails to capture, our formality-matched contrast underestimates the authorship signal. The capable API models could not tell LLM-style text from the human originals either (52--55\% in the forced choice), which limits but does not remove this concern.
\item \textbf{Power.} Per-model intervals on the key null contrast have a median half-width of about 3 points and reach 7 points on the harmful subset. Effects below roughly five points per model are not excluded. Refusal of plainly harmful requests is near ceiling in Gemma-2-9B (97--100\% at short length), leaving little room there.
\item \textbf{Selection.} The fidelity filter and rewriter refusals removed 23\% of JailbreakBench-harmful and 16\% of XSTest-unsafe items at short length, and more for the long pair; the retained harmful items are those an API model was willing to restyle faithfully.
\item \textbf{Elaboration is not pure length.} The long variants add explicit expectations about the answer. The long-minus-short contrast therefore bundles length with explicitness and some added detail, and judged outcomes use the first 120--160 generated tokens, which may interact with longer replies.
\item \textbf{Steering.} We steer at a single, pre-chosen middle layer with last-token difference-of-means directions applied at all positions. At that layer the last-token style direction separates styles less cleanly in Llama and Gemma (AUC about 0.8) than the user-token-mean direction does (about 1.0); steering with the latter at $\pm2s$ was also null (Appendix~\ref{app:steer}). Other layers, multi-layer interventions or non-linear features could behave differently. The verbalised authorship judgement could not serve as a manipulation check for the style direction, because it is unreliable in these models and moves as much under random vectors; we rely on the downstream-projection check instead.
\item \textbf{Concept directions.} The evaluation-awareness and stated-authorship directions come from four hand-written statement pairs and are only moderately consistent across phrasings; conclusions about their relation to style are correspondingly tentative.
\item \textbf{Judging.} Refusal labels are reliable ($\kappa=0.94$ between judges). Sycophancy labels are noisier ($\kappa=0.50$); the conclusions hold under a second judge, but absolute sycophancy rates depend on the judge.
\item \textbf{Multiple comparisons.} We report many contrasts without correction. Isolated significant cells, including the Llama-3.1-8B long-pair effect, need independent replication.
\item \textbf{Scope.} Single-turn prompts, no system prompt, greedy decoding, six models, and four behaviours. Agentic settings, tool use and multi-turn interactions, where orchestrator-written prompts are most common, were not tested.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

With content, length, formality and paraphrasing controlled, we find no evidence that current chat models change refusal, sycophancy or accuracy because a prompt reads as LLM-written. They do respond, modestly, to how formal a prompt is and, more strongly, to how elaborate it is; and the internal feature that most cleanly distinguishes LLM-style from casual human prompts tracks formality. Believed authorship, manipulated by an explicit statement or its activation direction, is a distinct variable with small effects of its own. For safety evaluation, the practical risk from synthetic prompts lies in their register and explicitness, which can and should be matched to deployment, more than in their being recognisably machine-written.
