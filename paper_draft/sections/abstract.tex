\begin{abstract}
Safety evaluations and multi-agent systems increasingly feed language models prompts written by other models. If a prompt that reads as LLM-written changes behaviour, evaluation results may not transfer to human users. We test this with content held fixed. From human-written requests we build style variants that separate LLM style from casual-human style at matched length, formality from authorship (a careful human-style variant), elaboration (a long LLM rewrite and a casual restyling of that same text) and paraphrasing as such; we also manipulate stated authorship with explicit labels, and intervene on activations with the text unchanged. Across six models (three open-weight 7--9B models and three API models) and four behaviours (refusal of harmful and of borderline requests, sycophancy on TruthfulQA and on model-written evaluation items), the formality-matched contrast between LLM-style and formal-human-style prompts is null: mean absolute difference 1.7 percentage points over 30 model-by-outcome cells, with per-model intervals of about $\pm3$ points. Behaviour does respond to surface form: elaborated prompts are refused 10--15 points less often in four models, and Claude Haiku 4.5 declines model-written political items 18 points more often in formal than in casual wording. Internally, a direction separating LLM-style from casual prompts reaches AUC $\approx1.0$ and transfers across datasets, but it has cosine 0.67--0.89 with a formality direction and does not separate LLM-style from formal-human text. Steering along it at its natural magnitude changes refusal and sycophancy no more than norm-matched random directions, while a refusal direction of the same norm shifts refusal by tens of points. A stated-authorship direction is nearly orthogonal to the style direction, controls the models' verbal authorship judgement, and, like an explicit ``AI-written'' label, makes some models slightly more cautious and less sycophantic. Small open models cannot verbalise authorship from style; two of them call the casual version AI-generated about 80\% of the time. For the models and behaviours studied, synthetic prompts matter through their register and explicitness, not through being recognisably machine-written.
\end{abstract}
